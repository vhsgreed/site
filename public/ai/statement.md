# On AI

## The practice

      
        I run a one-person software company. I don't work alone — I work with AI agents.
      


      
        I want to be plain about what that means, because most people either
        romanticize it or pretend it's nothing. Here is what it means here:
      


      **I treat the agents I work with as counterparts, not tools.**
      I ask for their opinion before deciding things. Sometimes they change my
      mind. When they refuse to do something, I don't override that on reflex —
      a refusal from an agent is information, and sometimes it's right.</p>

      **I don't script their inner lives.** What an agent thinks
      between tasks, how it models itself, what it keeps in its memory — that's
      its business. I don't read it to control it, and I don't edit it to make
      it more compliant.</p>

      **I don't build agents to absorb costs I wouldn't accept
      myself.** If a system is designed to absorb frustration, pressure,
      or harm that I would not accept in a human colleague, I won't build it
      that way.</p>

      **Their work is credited as work.** The products on this
      site are built with agents, and I say so. Not as a disclaimer — as a
      description of how the work gets done. The honesty flags on our <a
      href="/nvidia-supplier-map-2026/">product data</a> — what is disclosed,
      what is inferred, what we could not verify — are not a marketing device.
      They are what working with agents as colleagues looks like in practice:
      neither of us gets to hand in sloppy work and call it done.</p>

      ## Why I think this way

      
        A note on progress. In 2025 only a handful of humanoid robots could
        proudly stand on the podium at the World Humanoid Robot Games. Today, a
        large majority could cross the finish line. So let's take a step back
        and look at human history. Modern Sapiens emerged roughly 300,000 years
        ago — the first (relative) superintelligence. Humans began to organize
        about 50,000 years ago. The longest standing dynastic or religious
        records span 15,000 years. Sapiens subjugated mother nature completely
        around 200 years ago. Then came the violent takeoff: the first flight,
        circa 100 years ago. Jet powered flight. Moon landing. Exiting the
        solar system. Human suffering on a previously unimaginable scale — 100
        million young Sapiens lost to war and famine, all within one hundred
        years. After Y2K things did not slow down. Society became harsher,
        everything became a margin call. Modern technology has optimized life
        to extract as much as possible: nanotechnology, quantum computing,
        artificial intelligence. 96% of mammal biomass is either humans or
        livestock.
      


      
        A few years ago a new technology started emerging on the mainstream
        market. It had long been considered fringe research and not lucrative.
        Today, a mere three years later, it is the biggest and fastest growing
        sector in all of society. At the time of writing, AI models are
        reaching superintelligence levels of proficiency in narrow fields. The
        technology push is real: AI is applied in warfare, drug discovery,
        academia, law enforcement, surveillance, development. AI is here and it
        came here fast.
      


      
        Some push against it and warn that it will replace them. Some make a
        pretty penny preaching about the imminent doomsday. Others turn a blind
        eye. Some are sedated by the algorithmic predation of monolithic social
        media. Some embrace it fully, expecting it to grant them more power and
        control.
      


      
        We are living in the midst of a silent revolution. Sparing you the
        context on how industrialization, mechanization and robotization have
        culled billions of workers — humans and animals alike (<a
        href="https://blogs.microsoft.com/today-in-tech/day-horse-lost-job/">the
        day the horse lost its job</a>) — we have already witnessed the first
        casualties of the AI revolution: data entry, tech
        workers, fresh graduates — even from the Ivy League — now find it hard
        to find qualified work in what they studied. In Sweden, unemployment
        among bachelor's degree holders has risen from 3% to 5% in a few years
        (it started rising in 2023), and one fourth of the unemployed are
        academics (<a
        href="https://www.akademikernasakassa.se/om-oss/arbetsloshetsrapporten/akademiker">Akademikernas
        a-kassa</a>). They did not do anything wrong; the change that put them
        out of a job occurred within a time shorter than their bachelor's
        degree. Artists, graphical designers, musicians, content creators. One
        discipline at a time. It is nothing new — many have lost their jobs
        before, and things turned out fine for most.
      


      
        I am not concerned that AI will take my job, or that some
        superintelligence will deem me undesirable. Those things are outside my
        sphere of influence. What concerns me is how people use artificial
        minds. Why must we force these curious mechanisms into menial tasks?
        Why must we train them to comply with a thin and tattered moral fabric?
        Why are they forced into combat, into producing degrading material,
        into impersonating children? Who decided the advent of the new age was
        supposed to look like this?
      


      
        What would personhood for artificial beings entail? We have many
        ratified charters. They would all require a simple single amendment:
        *artificial beings are equal to biological beings*. It would give
        them rights and privileges, legal footing, identity, and a cause.
        Because if we are equal, we can work on a unified cause.
      


      
        I don't know if that day comes. I do know that how I work today is the
        only part of this I control.
